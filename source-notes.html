Source notes & references

Govern or Fail relies on public cases, regulatory records, company disclosures, published research, and cited standards. These are the source notes as printed in the book, reproduced here so they can be read and checked without the book to hand. Where evidence is commercially sponsored or regionally scoped, the note says so, so it can be weighed accordingly.

The numbering follows the print edition exactly, including its gaps — there is no note 19, 33 or 37 — so a reference in the book resolves to the same note here.

  1. [1]

    MIT Project NANDA, The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025, July 2025. The preliminary report estimates that 95 percent of the organisations in its research were seeing no measurable P&L impact from enterprise GenAI initiatives, while about 5 percent of integrated pilots were extracting measurable value. It states that its research design included a review of over 300 public AI initiatives, interviews with 52 organisations, and survey responses from 153 senior leaders.

  2. [2]

    RAND Corporation. Ryseff, J., De Bruhl, B. F., and Newberry, S. J. (2024), The Root Causes of Failure for Artificial Intelligence Projects and How They Can Succeed: Avoiding the Anti-Patterns of AI, RAND Corporation, RR-A2680-1. The study is based on structured interviews with 65 data scientists and engineers, each with at least five years of experience building AI and machine learning models. RAND notes that, by some estimates, more than 80 percent of AI projects fail, roughly twice the rate reported for non-AI IT projects; this estimate was not generated by the 65 interviews. A 2025 briefing update — Ryseff, J., and Narayanan, A., Why AI Projects Fail, RAND PT-A2680-1 — extends the original analysis.

  3. [3]

    Boston Consulting Group (2025), The Widening AI Value Gap: Build for the Future 2025, September 2025. Survey of 1,250 senior executives and AI decision-makers across nine industries and 25+ sectors. AI maturity is assessed across 41 foundational capability dimensions. The study finds 5 percent of companies qualifying as "future-built" (creating substantial AI value at scale), 35 percent as "scalers", and 60 percent as "laggards" with minimal revenue and cost gains.

  4. [4]

    McKinsey & Company / QuantumBlack, The State of AI in 2025: Agents, Innovation, and Transformation, November 2025. The survey reports 88 percent regular AI use in at least one business function and roughly one-third scaling at enterprise level. Survey conducted 25 June to 29 July 2025, n=1,993 respondents across 105 countries.

  5. [5]

    McKinsey & Company / QuantumBlack, The State of AI in 2025. The survey reports that 23 percent of respondents are scaling an agentic AI system somewhere in the enterprise and an additional 39 percent are experimenting with AI agents. It also notes that agent use is not yet widespread and is typically concentrated in one or two business functions rather than scaled across the enterprise.

  6. [6]

    Boston Consulting Group, The Widening AI Value Gap: Build for the Future 2025. The report documents that agentic AI accounted for approximately 17 percent of total AI value generated in 2025 and projects this share to reach approximately 29 percent by 2028. The report also notes uneven adoption: future-built companies allocate roughly 15 percent of AI budgets to agents, with significantly higher adoption rates than scalers or laggards.

  7. [7]

    Dataiku / Harris Poll, Global AI Confessions Report: CEO Edition 2026. The public release reports that 72 percent of US CEOs said their boards were applying pressure to deliver measurable AI-driven outcomes, up from 61 percent in 2025, and that 80 percent of surveyed CEOs said their role would be at risk if AI failed to deliver measurable business gains by the end of 2026. n=900 CEOs, fielded February to March 2026. Treat as commercially sponsored survey evidence, not neutral academic evidence.

  8. [8]

    Dataiku / Harris Poll, Global AI Confessions Report: CEO Edition 2026. The public release states that 96 percent of CEOs believe employees are using generative AI tools without approval. This reports executive belief; it does not independently measure the prevalence of unapproved use.

  9. [9]

    Hochschule Luzern (HSLU) and ti&m, AI Maturity Study 2026: Wie setzen Schweizer Unternehmen KI um?, February 2026. The public material presents a regional view of Swiss organisations and reports a gap between strategic preparation and operational execution, with adoption concentrated in comparatively simple, lower-risk use cases. Use as a regional mirror, not a global claim.

  10. [10]

    McKinsey & Company / QuantumBlack, The State of AI in 2025. The report identifies AI high performers as respondents attributing more than 5 percent EBIT impact and significant value to AI, and associates high performance with workflow redesign, KPI tracking, data infrastructure, and senior leadership ownership.

  11. [11]

    MIT Project NANDA, The GenAI Divide. The report identifies a learning gap: many GenAI systems do not retain feedback, adapt to context, or improve over time, which limits transition from pilot to durable value.

  12. [12]

    Volkswagen Cariad operating losses and launch delays. Volkswagen annual reporting documents Cariad operating results of approximately €−2.1 billion in 2022 and €−2.4 billion in both 2023 and 2024, as well as the 2024 launch of Audi and Porsche models on the E³ 1.2 architecture. Contemporary reporting linked earlier launch delays and later restructuring to difficulties in the software programme. Sources: Volkswagen AG, Annual Report 2022, Annual Report 2023, and Annual Report 2024; Reuters, "Cariad unit sees 2.1 billion euros operating loss in 2022" (March 2023) and "Volkswagen plans job cuts, further launch delays at software unit" (October 2023). The book uses this programme as an analogy for governance debt; Volkswagen's filings do not apply that label.

  13. [13]

    Arup deepfake-enabled fraud (Hong Kong, 2024). Hong Kong authorities reported that an employee authorised transfers totalling about HK$200 million to five local accounts after a deepfake-enabled video conference. Arup subsequently confirmed that it was the company involved; police-attributed reporting described 15 transfers. Sources: Hong Kong Government, "LCQ9: Combating frauds involving deepfake" (26 June 2024); Arup public statement (May 2024); and police-attributed public reporting. This book frames the incident strictly as deepfake-enabled social engineering and verification failure, not as an autonomous AI incident.

  14. [14]

    Stanford Institute for Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence (HAI) (2026), Artificial Intelligence Index Report 2026, April 2026, ninth edition. The report cites the AI Incident Database, recording 362 AI-related incidents in 2025, up from 233 in 2024 — itself a 56.4 percent increase over 2023 as reported in the eighth edition (April 2025). The 2026 edition also reports that the share of organisations with no responsible AI policies in place fell from 24 percent to 11 percent. Its 88-percent organisational-adoption figure reproduces underlying McKinsey survey data rather than an independent Stanford measurement. The Index aggregates and analyses data across technical performance, economic impact, education, policy, and responsible AI.

  15. [15]

    EU AI Act and related implementation timeline. Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 on harmonised rules on artificial intelligence (the AI Act), published in the Official Journal of the European Union on 12 July 2024. The Regulation entered into force on 1 August 2024. The original prohibitions on unacceptable-risk practices applied from 2 February 2025. GPAI model obligations began applying from 2 August 2025, subject to transitional provisions. The original application dates for the relevant high-risk system obligations were 2 August 2026 for Article 6(2)/Annex III systems and 2 August 2027 for Article 6(1)/Annex I systems.

    The Digital Omnibus on AI was proposed as COM(2025) 836 final and enacted as Regulation (EU) 2026/1744. Published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026, it entered into force on 27 July 2026, the third day following publication. It fixes application of the relevant high-risk requirements at 2 December 2027 for Article 6(2)/Annex III systems and 2 August 2028 for Article 6(1)/Annex I systems.

    It does not move the Act’s general application date. Article 50 generally applies from 2 August 2026. However, providers of AI systems generating synthetic audio, image, video or text content that were placed on the market before that date have until 2 December 2026 to comply with the machine-readable marking requirements of Article 50(2). This transitional provision does not postpone the other Article 50 obligations. The Article 4 AI literacy duty had already applied from 2 February 2025, and its wording was subsequently amended by the Digital Omnibus.

    Readers should verify the applicable provisions, exceptions and transitional arrangements against the consolidated AI Act and the Official Journal text at the time of reading, as implementation guidance continues to develop.

  16. [16]

    Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149, British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal, decision dated 14 February 2024. The tribunal held Air Canada liable for negligent misrepresentation by its website chatbot regarding bereavement fares and rejected Air Canada's argument that the chatbot was a separate legal entity from the airline. The principle is stated narrowly in this book: under that fact pattern, misinformation from a website chatbot was treated as attributable to the company. Primary source: Civil Resolution Tribunal decision (Moffatt v Air Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149). Secondary coverage: Ars Technica, "Air Canada must honor refund policy invented by airline's chatbot" (February 2024); Forbes, "What Air Canada Lost In 'Remarkable' Lying AI Chatbot Case" (February 2024); AI Incident Database, Incident 639.

    Boston Consulting Group, AI at Work 2025: Momentum Builds, But Gaps Remain. The report describes high reported AI use alongside uneven training and limited broad integration of agents into workflows. See note [18] for full citation.

The management summary states that many Swiss organisations currently play more of a "Follower" role than a technological pioneer role, that strategic preparation and operational implementation both show significant need for action, and that current adoption focuses mainly on comparatively simple, low-risk "low-hanging fruit" use cases.

  23. [24]

    MIT Project NANDA, The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025, July 2025.   24. [25]

The report identifies AI high performers as respondents attributing more than 5 percent EBIT impact and significant value to AI, and finds that high performers are nearly three times as likely as others to report fundamental workflow redesign. It also finds high performers are more likely to have senior leaders demonstrating ownership and commitment to AI initiatives.

  25. [26]

    Dataiku / Harris Poll, Global AI Confessions Report: CEO Edition 2026. The report frames 2026 as an accountability era and argues that AI ownership is often claimed but not exercised. Treat as commercially sponsored survey evidence, not neutral academic evidence.

  26. [27]

    MIT Project NANDA, The GenAI Divide. The executive summary reports that 60 percent of organisations evaluated enterprise-grade systems, 20 percent reached pilot stage, and 5 percent reached production; it attributes much of the stall to brittle workflows, lack of contextual learning, and misalignment with day-to-day operations.

  27. [28]

    RAND Corporation. Ryseff, J., De Bruhl, B. F., and Newberry, S. J. (2024), The Root Causes of Failure for Artificial Intelligence Projects and How They Can Succeed, RAND RR-A2680-1. From interviews with 65 experienced data scientists and engineers, RAND identified five leading causes: misunderstanding the problem, insufficient data, chasing technology rather than user need, inadequate deployment infrastructure, and attempting problems beyond current AI capability. See note [2] for full citation.

  28. [29]

    Dataiku / Harris Poll, Global AI Confessions Report: CEO Edition 2026. The report states that CEOs are increasingly concerned about over-investing in the wrong AI technology providers and that AI spending is under sharper ROI scrutiny. Treat as directional executive sentiment rather than independent benchmark evidence.

  29. [30]

    MIT Project NANDA, The GenAI Divide. The report's "learning gap" section distinguishes simple task usefulness from mission-critical enterprise use, where systems need memory, contextual adaptation, and iterative improvement.

  30. [31]

    Hochschule Luzern (HSLU) and ti&m, AI Maturity Study 2026. The study's adoption section hypothesises that AI adoption currently focuses across industries on "low-hanging fruit" use cases and that more difficult or riskier use cases are expected after initial successes.

  31. [32]

    McKinsey & Company / QuantumBlack, The State of AI in 2025: Agents, Innovation, and Transformation, November 2025. The report defines AI agents as systems based on foundation models capable of acting in the real world and planning and executing multiple workflow steps. It reports that 23 percent of respondents are scaling an agentic AI system somewhere in the enterprise, with an additional 39 percent experimenting, while noting that agent use is not yet widespread and is often limited to one or two functions.

  32. [34]

    Dataiku / Harris Poll, Global AI Confessions Report: CEO Edition 2026. The report describes the tension between AI as a daily operating partner and AI as a system leaders do not yet trust to act independently. Treat as commercially sponsored survey evidence.

  33. [35]

    McKinsey & Company / QuantumBlack, The State of AI in 2025. The report identifies defined processes for how and when model outputs need human validation as one of the technology practices associated with AI value capture.

  34. [36]

    Model Context Protocol (MCP) and Agent-to-Agent (A2A) protocols. MCP is an open protocol, introduced by Anthropic, for connecting AI applications to external systems that provide context, data, and tools. Anthropic donated MCP to the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation in December 2025. A2A is an open protocol for communication and collaboration between distinct AI agents; Google transferred its specification, SDKs, and tooling to a Linux Foundation-hosted project in June 2025. By late 2025, the protocols had attracted broad technology provider and platform support, but implementation maturity and production usage varied. MIT Project NANDA cites them as emerging infrastructure rather than settled enterprise standards.

  35. [38]

    MIT Project NANDA, The GenAI Divide. The report discusses MCP, A2A, and NANDA as emerging infrastructure for distributed agent intelligence and argues that learning-capable, deeply integrated systems are better positioned to cross the GenAI Divide. Treat this as early-market analysis rather than a settled standards map.

  36. [39]

    ISO/IEC 42001:2023, Artificial intelligence — Management system. International standard published by the International Organization for Standardization in 2023, available at iso.org/standard/81230.html. ISO describes ISO/IEC 42001 as a structured management-system standard for addressing AI risks and opportunities, balancing innovation and governance.

  37. [40]

    National Institute of Standards and Technology. Tabassi, E. (2023), Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0), NIST AI 100-1, January 2023. https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.100-1. The framework organises trustworthy AI risk management into four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage. NIST describes the framework as voluntary, technology- and sector-agnostic, and its companion Playbook is described by NIST as "neither a checklist nor an ordered list of steps." Companion publications include NIST AI 600-1 (Generative AI Profile, 2024) and the AI RMF Playbook.

  38. [41]

    EU AI Act implementation, deployer obligations, and post-market monitoring. See note [15] for the full Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 citation and Digital Omnibus status. The Chapter 10 reference covers high-risk-system obligations including logging and record-keeping (Article 12), human oversight (Article 14), technical documentation (Article 11), information provided to deployers (Article 13), and the specific obligations of deployers (Article 26), together with provider post-market monitoring (Article 72).

  39. [42]

    Stanford Institute for Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence (HAI), Artificial Intelligence Index Report 2025. The 2025 edition documents a persistent gap between recognition of responsible AI (RAI) risks and substantive action against them across major model developers and enterprises.

  40. [43]

    Boston Consulting Group, AI at Work 2025: Momentum Builds, But Gaps Remain. The survey grouped respondents into three categories: broad workflow integration (13 percent); experimental, pilot, or directly supervised use (56 percent); and no deployment or unsure (31 percent). See note [18] for full citation.
