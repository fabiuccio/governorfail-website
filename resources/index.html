Chapter 10 · the Tuesday exercise in Chapter 12

The customer challenge test

The cleanest way to find the gap is to go looking for it. Take one recent AI-supported decision, recommendation, action, or escalation. Assume it is disputed — by a customer, an employee, a regulator, an auditor, or a board committee. Then work through the questions an investigator would ask.

  1. Purpose and ownership

    Can the organisation show the approved purpose of the system, and identify the owner responsible for the outcome?

  2. Version, sources, and applicable rule

    Can it show which version produced the recommendation, what data or documents were used, and which policy or threshold applied?

  3. Human review

    Can it show whether human review occurred, and what the reviewer actually saw?

  4. Blast radius and correction

    Can it show whether similar decisions were affected, and what corrective action would follow if the decision were wrong?

How to read the result. If answering depends on missing records, undocumented handoffs, or somebody's memory rather than a defined evidence-retrieval process, the organisation cannot reliably demonstrate operational governance.

What the test exposes is a missing operating layer, and the requirement is narrower than it sounds: a production route that applies approved boundaries and creates the evidence policy and process require, without depending on each project team to reconstruct it after a challenge arrives.

Running it as the Tuesday exercise

Chapter 12 runs the same test before anyone challenges anything, and calls it the shortest honest test in the book. Take one AI-supported decision from the last quarter — a recommendation that was acted on, a customer response that was sent, a record that was updated — and ask the team to reconstruct it. Which system, which owner, which sources, which version, which human, which boundary. Time-box the initial attempt to an afternoon. The result shows what the team can reconstruct and where further work is needed.

The test ignores the comfort of aggregate governance entirely. It does not ask whether a conforming management system was implemented within scope. It asks whether one decision can be defended from approved purpose through runtime action to corrective response. None of which reduces the value of certification — the claim simply has to stay inside its scope.

Chapter 9

The technical control test

None of this requires an executive to read implementation line by line. It requires one test, applied to one material action: follow that action all the way through.

  1. Source and provenance

    Identify the source of the information used, and where it came from.

  2. Identities

    The user, service, and agent identities involved.

  3. Permissions and credentials

    The permissions and credentials available to the system at the time.

  4. Components and handoffs

    Every component and agent handoff along the path.

  5. Enforcement point

    The point where policy, approval, and transaction limits were enforced.

  6. Continuous trace

    The continuous trace through outcome and human intervention.

  7. Recovery path

    The path for finding affected actions, stopping behaviour, rolling back, and confirming remediation.

How to read the result. If the briefing cannot follow one action across all of those, the four layers are not connected, whatever the diagram shows.

Chapter 12

The four prerequisites

The book does not offer a methodology. But four conditions have to be true before any methodology is worth attempting. They are gating conditions, not a maturity model, and they come down to four verbs.

Discover

Discover the real estate

The official register is not the estate. Discovery has to find the systems, embedded features, agents, integrations, and employee tools that make, influence, or accelerate decisions, before risk can be prioritised at all.

Assign

Assign accountable ownership

Every material system needs one business owner answerable for the outcome, and named owners for process, service, data, model or agent, control plane, risk, and technology provider relationship.

Define

Define operational boundaries

Permitted data, tools, actions, approvals, escalation, evidence, and response have to become operating conditions that make behaviour auditable, bounded, and recoverable — not policy aspirations.

Build

Build the governed route

The production path has to enforce common control, preserve evidence, support recovery, and remain usable enough that teams choose it under pressure.

How to read the result. None of the four is an operating model. Without all four, any methodology starts from an ungovernable surface.

Chapter 11 · Appendix A

The scenario model

This is illustrative scenario logic. It is not a forecast, a benchmark, an expected outcome, or an estimate of total cost of ownership.

Every value below is a scenario assumption documented in Appendix A, and every one should be replaced with your own estimates before anyone acts on it. The sources cited in the book do not establish a precise governance velocity curve, and none of them tests the numbers here. The inference that reusable controls can change deployment economics is the author's, and should be evaluated as scenario logic rather than as an empirical finding.

The model loads with the book's base case so you can reproduce Figures 11.1 and 11.2, then change anything.

Two quantities are kept separate, because merging them is how this argument usually goes wrong. Deployment effort is cumulative labour in person-weeks — not elapsed calendar time. A governed deployment can consume less total effort and still take longer if a central review or platform team becomes a bottleneck; the model supports a claim about slope, not about schedule. Maintenance cost is a separate monthly quantity as the estate grows.

Deployment effort (cumulative person-weeks)

The mechanism underneath is reuse, and it is the condition everything else depends on. Data-boundary patterns, access routes, evidence conventions, review rules, and technology provider decisions reduce marginal work only when the next use case can actually inherit them. Without reuse, the higher initial effort is not an investment — it is just overhead.

Where this breaks

It is as important to say when the governed path does not win, because a conditional argument that never states its failure conditions is not conditional.

  • A crossover may never occur. It may fail to occur when reuse is weak, when systems are too heterogeneous to share controls, when the platform carries excessive fixed cost, when local exceptions recreate project-by-project work, or when teams simply cannot use the governed route. Raise the fixed platform cost above and watch it disappear.
  • Centralisation can destroy the benefit outright. When approval and platform functions operate as a queue rather than as infrastructure, lower cumulative effort coexists quite comfortably with longer elapsed time — and the delivery teams experiencing that queue will be correct to say governance slowed them down.
  • The crossover point is not a finding. Deployment three is the arithmetic consequence of the assumed 12-versus-24 starting point and the different marginal increments. Change either and the crossover moves.
  • The model excludes what it cannot generalise. Reputational damage, customer churn, opportunity cost of frozen roadmaps, sector-specific enforcement penalties, revenue impact from faster deployment cadence, and the commercial upside of trust in procurement are all left out. Those exclusions make the model less dramatic but more defensible.

The claim survives only where reuse is real. Test the model against deployment count, reuse rate, governance latency, platform cost, system heterogeneity, adoption of the shared route, and the maintenance burden of both common and local components.
