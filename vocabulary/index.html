Vocabulary

Glossary

This is Appendix B of Govern or Fail, reproduced in full. It defines the terms used in the book. It avoids proprietary framework names and focuses on operational vocabulary.

A2A (Agent-to-Agent Protocol)
An emerging protocol pattern for communication and coordination between agents. In this book, A2A is discussed as part of the broader move toward interoperable agentic systems, not as a guarantee of governance by itself.
Agentic AI
AI systems that can plan, use tools, retrieve information, call external systems, and carry work across multiple steps. The governance issue is not whether the system is conscious or autonomous in a philosophical sense. It is whether it can act operationally.
AI governance
The organisational discipline of keeping AI systems aligned to business intent, bounded by controls, monitored in production, and accountable when behaviour changes or fails.
AI registry
A maintained record of AI systems, owners, purposes, data access, technology providers, model dependencies, risk classification, and evidence status. A registry is only useful if it reflects the actual estate.
Audit trail
The evidence needed to reconstruct what happened: inputs, retrieved sources, model or prompt version, tool calls, output, human intervention, policy enforcement, and change history where relevant.
Behavioural drift
A change in the behaviour of an AI system over time, caused by model updates, prompt changes, data changes, workflow changes, user behaviour, or environmental context.
Certification
Independent third-party confirmation that a defined management system or other specified object meets stated requirements within a given scope. Certification is useful but not equivalent to runtime governance.
Challenge evidence
The specific records an organisation would need to defend one AI-supported decision if it were disputed: purpose, ownership, version, sources, applicable boundary, human intervention, and corrective action. See the customer challenge test in Chapter 10.
Data boundary
The approved scope of data an AI system may access, retrieve, process, or expose. Weak data boundaries are a common source of AI governance failure.
Demonstrated governance
Governance that can be shown with operational evidence, not merely described through policy, committee structure, or intent.
Governance debt
The future cost of deploying AI capability before ownership, boundaries, monitoring, evidence, and accountability have been designed into the system.
Governance theatre
The visible apparatus of governance without operational proof: policies, committees, registers, and statements that do not describe what actually happens in production.
Governed use
Use of an AI system through a production route that applies approved boundaries, preserves evidence, and has a named accountable owner — as distinct from use that is merely permitted by policy.
Human-in-the-loop
A control pattern in which human review or approval is part of the workflow. It is meaningful only if the reviewer has the information, time, authority, and independence needed to act.
ISO/IEC 42001
An international standard for AI management systems. In this book, it is treated as a useful management-system baseline, not a substitute for runtime control.
MCP (Model Context Protocol)
A protocol pattern for connecting AI systems to tools and data sources. In this book, MCP matters because governed connectivity is part of the production path.
Model versioning
The ability to know which model version, prompt version, configuration, or relevant runtime state produced a given output or action.
Prompt injection
An attack or failure pattern in which malicious or unintended instructions enter the context of an AI system and alter its behaviour.
RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)
A pattern in which a model retrieves external information and uses it to produce an answer. RAG improves grounding but introduces governance questions about source quality, access control, indexing, and evidence.
Runtime control layer
The production route through which AI access, data boundaries, logging, monitoring, policy enforcement, and evidence creation operate. The phrase describes a function, not a product name.
Shadow AI
AI tools, features, integrations, or workflows used outside official governance, procurement, architecture, or security visibility.
Workflow redesign
Changing how work is performed so AI becomes part of the process rather than a tool placed on top of the old process. The evidence base suggests this is one of the strongest differentiators of high performers.

Two axes from Chapter 5 are named in the book's Figure 5.1 rather than the glossary: governance discipline asks whether ownership, boundaries, monitoring, escalation, evidence, and change control actually operate — not whether they are documented; production value asks about measurable effect. They are a diagnostic lens for portfolio decisions, not a maturity model or scoring method.
